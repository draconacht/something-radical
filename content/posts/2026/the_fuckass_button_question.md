the premise is like this, "in front of you are two buttons. press the red button and you survive, press the blue button and you only survive if half of all people press the button."

i don't really know where the problem originates from. it was on a random twitter poll in 2023 and the discourse repeats itself whenever some checkmark decides to heat the leftovers for a quick buck. i'll approach this in a few steps to keep my sanity intact.

#### the correct answer is obviously {{COLOUR}}!

evidently not. all polls i saw on twitter were somewhere between 1:1 and 1:3 in favour of blue. i'm not going to check on lesswrong because i only have enough patience for psychopaths _without_ a masters degree.

#### the problem is only non-trivial because of the affects!

very many people have rephrased the problem with different affects; changing the button colours, rephrasing the problem to sound more threatening to red pickers, etc. this is a fine thing to do, and you can definitely say red is the correct option to press given different affects.

what i don't understand is why some people think that makes red the "objectively" correct choice. why would a different problem not have a different optimal answer?

one could very well argue that red choosers are also reaching that point through affective reasoning. after all, noticing certain mathematical properties of a problem is also a kind of prejudice that people are socially trained into. 

#### this sure suspiciously aligns with contemporary american politics colours

yes it does. i expect the results to be closer if you switched the colours actually.

#### what button would you press anyway?

blue.

#### you wouldn't jump into a blender?

yeah i wouldn't. that's a different problem. 

#### but it's the same problem?

the primary decider for the correct choice is how many people you expect to press blue. if you think it will be a large number, and will include people you care for, and it could plausibly cross the majority margin, pressing blue is the correct choice. 

when you change the phrasing, you end up with different priors. this is not simply a "mathematical" problem where you're trying to optimise your probability of survival.

#### what if i tweak the formulation to make the answer more ambiguous? that will shake your confidence!

the closer a dilemma is to being ambiguous, the more context you need to make a choice. same goes with choosing one of two flavours, political parties, what have you.

#### but there is _literally_ no risk to picking red!

picking red may very well be optimal if you're accounting for maximising individual utility without taking into account all of society. however, such a utility does not exist. most people choose to act good even when selfish actions have little to no risk of getting them in trouble. of course, you can argue this is just moral training and they will abandon it the moment the reward is _just_ mouthwatering enough. that's fine. this is evidently a scenario where people's moral reasoning sticks with them.

one may very well choose to press blue because they consider martyrdom to be aligned with their religious values. they may very well even jump into Roko's blender. calculated utility is hardly a metric of value in broader socio-cultural contexts. having to live with one's choice on the poll can itself be a factor in how a person feels about it.

there is also no real necessity to think of it as _risk_. there are prosocial and antisocial actions. as a heuristic prosocial actions tend to ensure better survival for species. if half of all people can co-ordinate to ensure _everyone_, regardless of their choices or beliefs survives, that's something prosocial that gets people riled up to act.

#### blue is a virtual signal!

enacting virtue signals virtue. you can disagree with those virtues, but it is a coherent choice.
#### i'm sure it will be different in poorer countries, where people have less reason to love each other!

are... you sure about that? social bonds tend to be less cohesive in developed nations. not to account for the effect of religious thought on a person's choice. of course, i wouldn't be sure if it goes one way or another either without evidence, but i don't think it's that straightforward.

#### thank you for pressing blue, unlike those evil red pressers!

not really. it's a reasonable choice to make if you expect a very small number of people to press blue. you'd be _wrong_ to expect that, but wrong isn't evil.

i will grant that red pickers corelate with evildoers in society (so do lesswrong users). but that's moreso because they're more likely to be in positions of relative power. put the average hippie blue presser arts major in a powerful position in a large company and they'll cause similar amounts of harm. 

not surprisingly, a lot of people in the red camp have some evil shit to say about blue pressers. some of it is how education is done. some of it is that people are prejudiced to see counter-intuitive answers as more intelligent (see prisoner's dilemma). some of it is algorithms boosting the loudest wrongest people (one really wonders how self-professed cold calculating mathematicians got suckered into paying for X premium).

#### does this even have anything to do with ethics? it's all logic...

ethics is logic. right and wrong choices don't exist in a vaccuum. they're contingent on the state of reality and everyone in it.

#### why would you choose the emotional answer over the logical answer anyway?

i don't see either answer as falling into those categories. this is a co-ordination problem. logic dictates you act in accordance with your prediction of other people's actions. if their actions are dependent on their emotions, your logic has to account for emotional realities. that said emotions can be independent of logic... so that's one point for team emotion, i guess?

#### are you suggesting that learning game theory is a cognitohazard?

_looks at my suspiciously one million dollar shaped pocket_.

#### so can we get rid of the red pressers so that the world is good again?

beats me. record-breaking famine isn't exactly a tempting offer.

#### i did the math and you're wrong...

assuming all people involved are rational self-interested utility maximisers? did you account for the utility of martyrdom? the utility of putting the shopping cart back when you're not rewarded to do it? the utility of upholding societal virtues? the utility of knowing when you sleep at night whether or not you contributed to saving humanity? did you account for most people not knowing game theory? no? come back when you're modelling human behaviour then. 

you can suggest how to maximise utility with expected means and probabilities. however, when people act, they act based on highly determistic factors. if you're not accounting for those biases because you want to assume all people are "rational actors", you will make for a terrible economist. economics is hard because humans aren't simple.
