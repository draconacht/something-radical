## the joy of cooking

there is a joy in cooking. both in the learning of new recipes and the repetition of existing ones. there is a joy to failing at cooking and eating dishes that are a tad overcooked or a tad undersalted. i'm not exactly sure where this comes from. one part of it is the joy of creating something, the same as creating a drawing or writing a post. there is something that exists now, that has not existed before, that was created by your hands. it is also the joy of being able to feed yourself, and feed yourself with something well-tasting and (hopefully) nutritious. many people i know have lived in environments at home, hostels, or work, where good food was a scarcity. having control over one's circumstances is empowering for people.

to add to this, eating food is a _necessity_ for human beings. this may seem obvious, but the topics of food and cooking are criminally underdiscussed in philosophy. for an inevitably of the human body on par with birth, love, and death, far less merit has been given to food and the processes around it than it's due.

cooking is contingency planning. if you depend on someone for your meals, and suddenly you can't depend on them, then you're in trouble. even if you can cook mediocre meals by yourself, it's a much better experience than waiting for a parent or family member or cook to arrive so you can have something to eat. of course in many cases you can order in in extreme circumstances, but that's not a solution for prolonged absence.

cooking is control. you may have eaten grilled chicken hundreds of times before. there may be some things you like about it, and some things you don't. maybe you would like it with a bit more spice, or some lemon, or more garlic. maybe you want to switch to a non-meat option but still desire the taste. you won't know exactly how you get what you want unless you try to cook it yourself. cooking teaches you exactly what ingredient imparts which flavour. i for one, did not know what unsalted rice tasted like until i tried to cook rice myself.

cooking is cultivative. much like art or sport, you start by sucking at it. no matter how skilled you might be in other things, in all likelihood you'll find yourself struggling at basic recipes. even besides cookbooks and online recipes, cooking relies on certain intuitions which need to be developed. the longer you practice cooking, the more these intuitions within you become instinct. and one fine day, you'll find that the onions are cooked just right without you paying much attention. at this point the skill is cultivated within you, and it stays with you much the same way balancing on a bicycle does.

cooking is collaboration. reading a recipe puts us in a one-way conversation with whoever prepared it for us. if it's a local resource it might be tailored for ingredients and tastes specific to the region. if it's from a family or friend it might be tailored to their specific needs and tastes. you will find yourself eventually synthesising those with your own idiosyncracies, and come up with something a very little special. chances are you will also share these with others. as eating is a universal experience, food is a universal topic of communication.

cooking is creativity (experimentation). cooking meals gives you the opportunity to try new flavours and combinations. sometimes an experiment can be as simple as removing an ingredient you don't have in stock. you may find that you quite like omelettes with onions but no tomatoes, or something else like that. sometimes it's substituting spice A for spice B. flavour is not the net sum of comprised ingredients, so you can mix and match a lot of things with surprisingly novel results.

## in-person grocery shopping

unlike the previous section, this is just a preference that i have and i don't present it with much normative weight. i do not like app-based shopping whatsoever. once the novelty of "you can get _anything_" wears off, there's very little offered by online commerce stores that you can't buy at a supermarket. of course, your mileage will vary a lot depending on how good the grocery stores near you are, and how accessible it is to reach those stores.

app stores (on web but far more so on mobile interfaces) are extremely noisy. looking at a list of items on a 6" screen restricts you to a very limited field of view compared to shopping in person. it doesn't help that apps are littered with advertisements, many of them designed to be as invisible as possible without violating laws.

scrolling through a list of items with two columns makes much worse use of visual parallelism / peripheral view than looking at a full shelf. you're likely to filter through options much more quickly, and recall items you had forgotten. it's also more likely to introduce you to new options, because with apps you scroll through groceries till you find the thing that you want.

i've found in-person grocery stores to be better for trust. you know that you're not being priced differently for whichever operating system your phone uses. your bill may even end up a bit more expensive than on the app, but it will be a much more consistent price, and one that isn't subject to surge pricing, weather surcharges, or complicated ever-changing subscription plans.

all of these preferences come with a degree of falsifiability. we could, by all means, make a _good_ grocery shopping app. one that doesn't use dark patterns and has consistent pricing. the field of view problem is much better on a desktop interface. these are incidental problems. i have two essential objections with this system though.

i like being at grocery stores. i like seeing and holding things in their exact proportions before i buy them. who among us has not online-purchased the wrong quantity of an item before? i like the geometry of items all neatly aligned and stacked. when eggs are expensive, i can ask the shopkeeper why, and they tell me exactly what the reason is.

i do not like being served for things i can comfortably do myself. i'll admit the criteria for this are vague, and in many cases i'll break this rule because it would be more polite that way. for example, i prefer to return trays myself when leaving a restaurant, but in many places that would be impolite so i pass on it, even though it's convenient enough. for me getting groceries on the way home from work is a convenient affair. this has been the case for most of my life, though i'm sure there are people in different circumstances for whom it does not hold.

## the joy of code

much as producing food is creative labour, producing code is also creative labour. it may not be that way for many people's jobs (for both categories), but it is creative for the best of jobs in both professions. writing code is _writing_, and the application of language (moreso, patterns on top of basic semantics) is a creative process whether one is writing text or code.

coding provides control. if you can write code, you can control your computer to operate in a certain way. scripting spreadsheets allows you to automate boring calculations. writing scrapers gives you the power to do aggregate operations on web pages quickly (something you will come to value very quickly in the right kind of organisation). coding enables you to make a website far cheaper, lightweight, and more efficient than you would get out of Wordpress or Shopify or the like.

coding is cultivative. every language has a learning curve. scripting itself requires time to get hang of. for a long time everything you make will fail miserably (or sit on a shelf being unused). tools and techniques have nigh-infinite scope for refinement. mastery happens when your code surprises you pleasantly. i have found myself realising at many times that an arduous change in requirements is actually quite simple to implement because i had planned for this contingency, then forgotten about it. that to me is the hallmark of beautiful code, and arguably the whole point of the damn affair.

coding is collaborative. people by-and-large enjoy sharing their projects and learning from each other's work. many times i have run into an opaque project that is absolutely impenetrable outside the author's mind and un-runnable outside of the author's machine. some of the best projects i've seen have multiple active collaborators (though this does not guarantee quality), and rigorous review processes. people have a diversity of experiences, and a second contributor (no matter how unskilled) will often detect as many as 80% of the bugs and oversights in your code.

coding is creative and experimentative. every new project is an opportunity to execute things a little differently. the best game development studios constantly run internal and external experiments. deep feedback systems and flexible / modular code enable us to run many of these experiments. only through experiments you can really learn what works for users (and developers), and what doesn't. many times you are bound by resources, or idiotic constraints set by your upstream and downstream peers, or the problem space itself. constraints, though, are what build character. i found myself once stuck running a massive file processing job on an HDD. fortunately, i had enough RAM at the time to just load data batches into memory files and process though. i have found countless occasions to recommend this to other people who were stuck in the same situation as i.

there is one notable difference though. human beings don't have an essential biological need to code, so it isn't some sort of contingency planning when you're able to do it yourself. unless it's your job of course.

## LLM-driven software development, and other hindrances to joy

first disclaimer - the article so far has not been a build up to this punchline. i've been meaning to write down my thoughts on cooking for a while, and connecting them to _another_ creative endeavour of my life is its own talking point.

second disclaimer - i'm sure there are very nuanced opinions to be had on language models outside of the domain of software development. i'm not going to discuss that as i have no need for them outside of writing code. so i'm not interested in speaking about that.

very many times, people who are qualified cooks with many years of experience want to eat junk food, or cheap snacks. this is normal, and it's good that we can make space for such desires without being judgemental. cooking, like many essential biological needs, can become exhausting over time. likewise, many professional programmers also find themselves exhausted by their job, as one does.

- one remedy for this is to pay someone as little as possible to get the job done. one may order in food, and likewise one may use a cheap / free LLM to write some code for them and commit it as is (to the stomach). this minimises labour, but also agency in the task at hand.
- another is to rely on predigested labour. one may cook instant meals, which give a considerable amount of agency but may not be as healthy. one may likewise copy code from a friend or co-worker, and try to integrate it in their project somehow.

of course, none of these are in themselves unhealthy. in fact, a lot of healthy people will frequently rely on these. the problem starts when we these dominate over our ability to cultivate skill.

a person who can only make instant noodles is hardly a good chef. likewise a person who can only make CRUD apps with one from an assortment of frameworks is hardly a great developer. diversity of experiences builds expertise. i used Haskell briefly for one of my projects, and i recommend learning a functional language even if they don't need to use it (as was recommended to me). exposure to diverse paradigms shows you new possibilities to solve problems in novel ways that you may not have been aware of. quite a handful of computational geometry problems have applications in game development, and vice versa.

i find it also a shame that many workplaces actively deny such cultivation of skill. an office that values _delivery_ in denial of _learning_ is akin to a farm which only rewards reaping, not sowing. evaluation like this is only sustainable for the first harvest season. top-down pressures to show "LLM productivity increases" have forced many teams to adopt breakneck paces, compensating for them by taking on enormous amounts of tech debt. my worry for software engineering is not that AI will replace engineers altogether, but that AI-incentivised programming will create such enormous amounts of tech debt that developers will simply refuse to work on projects, stalling the pace of progress to a halt. it's a slow death by strangulation that many companies have suffered even prior to AI.

it's also no coincidence that hustle-centric startups with long shifts actively disincentivise their employees from learning how to cook. i've heard many stories of workplaces that give lucrative food vouchers (in partnership with the gig work food delivery platform of your choice) and provide dinners at office. any time spent learning how to cook is time spent not at the workplace. likewise any time spent learning how to code is also spent not at work. the lopsided incentive to reap profits is anathema to the process of learning. employees who are constantly stressed about making it to the next deadline will never choose to make decisions that help them in the longer run. this is ironically quite common in finance, which touts concepts like the time value of money and the importance of long-term planning. i find the effective elimination of the junior developer role to be baffling, seeing so many companies shirk from their responsibility of cultivating talent the moment they get the opportunity. this i believe to be a great moral failing of the present day.

professional utility notwithstanding, denying people the opportunity to learn is denying them the opportunity to gain control over their faculties. the unskilled programmer or cook will only do a task in the first way that comes to their mind. the skilled programmer or cook can do the same task in four different ways (something as simple as chopping an onion), and will choose the correct way for each situation. however, learning a new way to do task X is much more expensive than doing X the exact same way you have always done it. with the wrong incentives an employee will never choose to learn new ways to do a thing. this, then, is the nature of vibe coding. it is hamburger meat disguised as steamed hams. it repeats itself in unwarranted circumstances, and follows the path of least resistance unless it fails. but once a cook has been cultivated, they can cook the same meat in four different ways without having to learn it, because they have already learnt it. this gives them a far higher degree of adaptability.

an unrelated note is my distaste for closed-source code, non-disclosure agreements, and trade secrets. rarely have companies sunk because their codebase is open source, yet many choose to act as if this somehow this makes their business model more viable. if a moat is required to maintain profitability, then perhaps the idea was not profitable to begin with. but enacting this filling of moats will require broader professional-economic changes that i'm not in the mood to talk about. regardless, employees across companies love talking about their code and problems at conferences. i love conferences and open forums because they're great ways to get exposure to diverse ideas, and enables people to think outside the groupthink of their specific workplace. however for whatever reasons these interactions are unacceptable to enterprises as they stand, with most unwilling to even host a conference unless there's something in it for them.

## embracing amateurishness

AI-accelerated FOMO has led many in computer science to make outlandish claims about the necessity of velocity. one does not need to become an established developer in their first year of college, and in most cases they cannot. you do not need to get a job at Google or Microsoft or Amazon to be considered a good developer, and many a person in those companies actively hate their job. the rat race is a lie even when measured on its own terms. in the same way that one does not need to get into an IIT to become a good engineer (which would imply there are only some 5,000 employable engineers in India, which is a laughable claim), one does not need to start with a fancy job to lead a successful career and a happy life.

the industry of software engineering is hardly under threat. we made near-autonomous robots decades ago and many warehouses are still hiring. restaurants have existed since time immemorial and yet people so-frequently cook their own food. i find a very difficult time believing LLM tools are going to become _cheaper_ than human developers any time soon. every year it is argued that software engineering will end in some 18 months, and that goalpost is shifted by 12 months next year. this "criti-hype" has made many aspiring developers sabotage their education out of desperation. i have disliked the rat race from a very young age. _most_ of the most wonderful capable engineers i've met in person are not from IITs or NITs. they're from colleges i hadn't heard the names of till i met them. the rat-race navel-gazing has also been tremendously destructive to the process of hiring, but that's a different topic. rest assured, the bottom 90% hold up 90% of the sky.

amateur cooking and code have some obvious shortcomings to them which are nevertheless joyful to discover. an incorrectly salted dish is almost immediately noticeable. it's also hard to forget, and so people develop an intuition for salting a dish relatively quickly. learning is about being at these stages of intermediate mistakes, and allowing yourself to fall and stand up from them. things crumble and break, lump and tear. you taste the consequences of your actions, and it reminds you exactly where you fall short. _experiencing_ failure is a more holistic way of learning than _being told_ about failure is. this is why flash cards can be more effective than reading the same text ten times.

of course, the good cook rarely strays away from using technologies where it empowers them. a good non-stick pan, a silicon spatula, or a complex spice can be a good tool for the scrappy amateur cook. likewise the talented developer will gladly use sophisticated editors, forums, and scripts. they will also use quite frequently use LLMs in a way that augments their learning instead of hindering it (see Doctorow's centaurs and reverse centaurs). in particular, i think LLMs make for great search engine substitutes, and are great at pointing you to other resources to learn conventions from. they can also be great for fuzzy refactoring in scenarios where your type system doesn't accurately portray all references (a bit more sophisticated than the kind of the fuzzy text replace that exists in Jetbrains tools). the question to ask is, does this activity enable me to do something that i previously could not, or does it hinder me from something i'd like to learn? i believe most people are good judges of this when they're not under pressure.

as a closing point, there are very many 101 tutorials to software engineering, and there are very many professional guides for already-skilled developers. to a degree the same holds for cooking. here are some broad techniques that help to fill the gap between a novice and an expert:

- cheap beginner recipes - there's nothing shameful about starting by boiling eggs or using python. people who tout sophisticated complicated techniques often do so for ego reasons. there's a surprising lot you can do with the most basic approach to a goal, and it's good to build upwards from there.

- professional guidance - one of the best ways to get good at something is to talk to someone who is already good at said thing. this is obvious in general, but more pertinent for amateurs. you happen to be in a position where you know more than zero information, and don't necessarily have the ability to pick and choose sources with your own discretion. a good mentor can be most effective at this phase of learning.

- focus on intermediate steps - treat a simple component of a larger recipe as its own project. if you know how to make _masala_ for _masala dosa_, you can reuse a lot of that cooking process for other recipes. chopping and cooking potatoes / onions is the same in a lot of recipes.

- embrace failure and talk about it with others - failure teaches you how an experiment fails. this is important to learn, because you will be failing in many experiments throughout your life, even after becoming an expert. understanding and anticipating the modes of failure is learnt best if you let yourself fail. in many cases, your mentors or friends may have failed in the exact same way that you have. and if they haven't, even better, because now you've taught them about a new possibility to be wary of.
